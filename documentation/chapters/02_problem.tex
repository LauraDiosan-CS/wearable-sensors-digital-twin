\section{Problem definition}
\label{section:problemDefinition}

Wearable ECG is hard to use for three reasons:
\begin{itemize}
	\item \textbf{Noise.} Motion, muscle and electrode artefacts corrupt the signal, so its quality has to be checked before any analysis \cite{satija2018review}.
	\item \textbf{Volume.} A single lead at 250~Hz gives 21.6 million samples per day.
	\item \textbf{Inter-patient variability.} Classifiers tested on unseen patients do much worse, especially on supraventricular beats \cite{dechazal2004automatic}, which led to patient-specific models \cite{kiranyaz2016realtime}.
\end{itemize}

A digital twin is a model of one individual that is continually updated from their data and used for monitoring and decision support \cite{corralacero2020digital,niederer2021scaling}. Existing cardiac twins are biophysical models built from imaging and 12-lead ECG \cite{gillette2021framework}, data a wearable does not provide. We instead use a low-dimensional ECG model \cite{mcsharry2003dynamical} calibrated to the wearer's clean beats, together with learned models, split across the five agents of the course brief. The output is a report for a clinician; the system does not diagnose.

\paragraph{Why an intelligent algorithm?} Fixed filters and thresholds break down under noise and individual variation. On single-lead ambulatory ECG, a deep network exceeds the average cardiologist's $F_1$ score \cite{hannun2019cardiologist}. Learning is also needed for denoising, for forecasting heart rate and for calibrating the twin, which is an inverse problem. The agent layer decides which models to run and when the signal is too poor to trust.

\section{Formal problem statement}
\label{section:formal}

\paragraph{Signals.} For patient $p$ the wearable records $\tilde x_p[n] \in \R^{C}$ (usually $C=1$) at $f_s$~Hz, possibly with auxiliary signals $s_p[n]$ (optical heart rate, motion). We assume
\begin{equation}
\tilde x_p[n] = x_p[n] + \eta[n],
\label{eq:noise}
\end{equation}
where $x_p$ is the clean ECG and $\eta$ is baseline wander, muscle and electrode-motion artefact (the noise types of the MIT-BIH Noise Stress Test Database \cite{moody1984noise}) plus powerline interference. R peaks $r_k$ give RR intervals $RR_k = r_k - r_{k-1}$ and beats $b_k \in \R^{L \times C}$.

\paragraph{The twin.} The twin of patient $p$ at time $t$ is
\begin{equation}
\twin_p(t) = \big(\vartheta_p(t),\ \rho_p(t),\ \mathcal{H}_p(t)\big),
\label{eq:twin}
\end{equation}
with ECG model parameters $\vartheta_p$, rhythm state $\rho_p$ (mean heart rate, variability) and history of findings $\mathcal{H}_p$. In the McSharry model \cite{mcsharry2003dynamical} a point circles a unit limit cycle in the $(x,y)$ plane with angular velocity $\omega = 2\pi/RR$, and the ECG is its $z$ coordinate:
\begin{equation}
\dot z = -\sum_{i \in \{P,Q,R,S,T\}} a_i\, \Delta\theta_i \exp\!\left(-\frac{\Delta\theta_i^2}{2 b_i^2}\right) - \big(z - z_0(t)\big),
\qquad \Delta\theta_i = (\theta - \theta_i) \bmod 2\pi .
\label{eq:ecgsyn}
\end{equation}
Each wave is a Gaussian with angle $\theta_i$, amplitude $a_i$ and width $b_i$, so $\vartheta \in \R^{15}$; $z_0$ is a respiratory baseline. $g(\vartheta, RR)$ denotes one generated beat.

\paragraph{Sub-problems.} Each agent solves one sub-problem (Table~\ref{tab:subproblems}). Calibration (P4) estimates the patient's parameters from clean beats classified as normal, $\mathcal{K}_p$, with a prior around the population parameters $\vartheta_0$:
\begin{equation}
\hat\vartheta_p = \arg\min_{\vartheta}\ \sum_{k \in \mathcal{K}_p} \big\| b_k - g(\vartheta, RR_k) \big\|_2^2 + \lambda \big\| \vartheta - \vartheta_0 \big\|^2_{\Sigma^{-1}} .
\label{eq:calibration}
\end{equation}
Each new beat is compared with the beat the twin predicts,
\begin{equation}
d_k = \frac{\big\| b_k - g(\hat\vartheta_p, RR_k) \big\|_2}{\hat\sigma_p \sqrt{L}},
\label{eq:deviation}
\end{equation}
where $\hat\sigma_p$ is the patient's noise level, so $d_k \approx 1$ for typical beats. $d_k$ is an extra input to the arrhythmia detector.

\paragraph{Evaluation.} We use the inter-patient split of de Chazal et al.~\cite{dechazal2004automatic}: the 44 non-paced MIT-BIH records form two halves of 22 records, one for training and one for testing. Records 201 and 202 come from the same subject and stay together \cite{moody2001impact}. Noise robustness is tested by adding recorded noise \cite{moody1984noise} at several SNRs. Our hypothesis is that adding $d_k$ improves the detection of S and V beats at equal positive predictivity.
